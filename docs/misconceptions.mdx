# Misconceptions About IQ
This section aims to dispell popular myths regarding psychometrics and IQ.

---

## Misconceptions about *what* IQ actually measures

### "IQ tests only measure how good you are at taking IQ tests."
The claim that "IQ tests only measure how good you are at taking IQ tests" collapses when set beside a century’s worth of predictive-validity research. Scores on well-constructed intelligence tests generalise far beyond the test session, anticipating how easily people learn, solve novel problems and translate knowledge into real-world results, so much so that psychologists sometimes call g "the indispensable variable."[^gwern1]

#### Academic achievement
Meta-analyses spanning tens of thousands of students show that cognitive-ability measures are among the strongest single predictors of classroom performance. Kuncel, Hezlett and Ones (2004) found average corrected correlations in the mid-.30s to mid-.40s between general-ability tests and university GPA, with similar effects for degree completion and licensure success[^pubmed1]. These findings dovetail with educational data in In the Know, where higher-IQ pupils "learn more rapidly, make fewer errors and consistently out-score their classmates on standardized achievement tests". The book’s life-outcome inventory lists grades, literacy and persistence in school among the many positive correlates of IQ[^ubalt1]. In short, intelligence tests predict how well students will do long after the bubble sheet is handed in.

Workplace performance. The same pattern appears in employment. A landmark meta-analysis showed that, once statistical artifacts are removed, general mental ability correlates about r ≈ .58 with performance in the most complex jobs and r ≈ .23–.51 in less complex roles. An updated review of 100 years of selection research puts the mean validity of g at roughly .50 across all jobs and demonstrates that pairing an IQ test with an integrity test or structured interview drives multivariate validities into the .70s[^emil1]. These effect sizes translate into large economic gains for organisations because smarter employees master training faster, make fewer errors and sustain higher productivity.

Why it works. Intelligence predicts performance indirectly: people with higher g acquire job-specific knowledge and skills more quickly, and that knowledge, in turn, boosts output and accuracy. Meta-analytic work confirms that job knowledge mediates much of the g → performance link. Thus, the tests forecast potential for learning and adaptation—not just rote test-taking tricks.

Long-term life outcomes. Beyond school and work, higher IQ in youth forecasts later income, occupational prestige and employment stability. Strenze’s (2007) longitudinal meta-analysis found that childhood intelligence predicts educational attainment (r ≈ .56), occupational status (r ≈ .51) and income (r ≈ .40) decades later, even after family socioeconomic status is controlled


### "IQ just measures 'book smarts'; it says nothing about real-world ability."
Higher IQ predicts job performance, training speed, income, health literacy, and even longevity—outcomes well beyond classrooms.

### "All online IQ tests are fake and only an in-person administration is accurate."
Test

### "There is more than one intelligences and IQ tests only measure one, therefore they're inaccurate."
"Multiple intelligences" is a pedagogical theory; empirical factor analyses keep finding a strong general factor (g) captured well by IQ batteries.

### "Untimed IQ tests are more accurate than timed IQ tests."
Timed subtests exist, but composites blend speed with untimed reasoning, memory, and vocabulary measures.

### "IQ doesn't measure anything meaningful beyond 120 IQ and is only good at telling apart intellectual disability."
At the upper end, each additional SD still predicts rarer creative, academic, and technical accomplishments in longitudinal studies.

## Misconceptions about what *determines* a person's score

### "IQ scores are determined by how much schooling you've had."
Schooling can lift scores a bit, but twin and adoption studies show substantial heritable—and stable—individual differences even after controlling for education.

### "Because IQ correlates with socio-economic status, it's just measuring wealth."
Socio-economic status and IQ correlate (~0.3-0.4), but statistical controls show independent variance for each; the test isn't a proxy for parents' income.

### "IQ tests are culturally biased, so scores are meaningless."
Culture-fair items aren't perfect, but modern norms, adaptive testing, and item-response theory shrink group bias dramatically; predictive validity still holds within groups.

### "The Flynn effect shows that we are hundreds of points smarter than our ancestors"
Rising raw scores show environmental gains in certain sub-skills; once norms are re-standardized, relative rank ordering (the construct) remains robust.

### "Brain training apps can raise your IQ by 20-30 points, proving the IQ is trainable."
Most cognitive-training gains are task-specific and fade quickly; broad transfer large enough to spike IQ scores hasn't been replicated.

## Misconceptions about what an IQ score predicts or can't predict

### "Hard work, creativity, or emotional intelligence matter more, so IQ is obsolete."
Those traits add value, yet none matches IQ's breadth or predictive power for academic and technical learning curves; they're complements, not replacements.

### "I know successful low IQ people and unsuccessful high IQ people, so IQ must be fake."
IQ raises probabilities, not certainties. Life outcomes are multi-factorial—personality, opportunity, and luck all matter.

## Misconceptions regarding ethical and political critiques

### "IQ was invented to promote eugenics, so the whole system is biased."
Early misuse doesn't invalidate current psychometrics, any more than 19th-century medical errors invalidate today's medicine.

### "Because group averages differ, the whole test must be racist/sexist."
Group mean gaps say nothing about individual scores; moreover, predictive validity within each group is comparable.

## Miscellaneous Misconceptions

### "Richard Feynman was a genius but he only scored 125 IQ, proving IQ isn't that meaningful."

A common belief is that Feynman is 125 IQ. The test in which Feynman scored 125 was as an adolescent in high school, meaning his scores likely are not representative of his capabilities as an adult. We also cannot determine whether or not the test was a verbal test or a full-scale test, though it is heavily speculated it was a verbal test, meaning measurements of Feynman's strong fluid reasoning skills were likely neglected. 

> According to his biographer, in high school the brilliant mathematician Richard Feynman's score on the school's IQ test was a 'merely respectable 125' (Gleick, 1992, p. 30). It was probably a paper-and-pencil test that had a ceiling, and an IQ of 125 under these circumstances is hardly to be shrugged off, because it is about 1.6 standard deviations above the mean of 100. The general experience of psychologists in applying tests would lead them to expect that Feynman would have made a much higher IQ if he had been properly tested.

*John Carroll (1996), The Nature of Mathematical Thinking (p. 9)*

His IQ is most likely much higher than 125, but it's impossible to know by how much due to lack of information. It is common for people to use this outlier as a point against the accuracy of IQ; however, a lot of the details regarding Feynman's score are questionable.

### "Chess skill is strongly correlated with IQ."

It's not, not strongly that is. Fluency in chess is more indicative of training and taking advantage of the greater neuroplasticity and synaptic pruning one has in their younger years, although working memory and processing speed are likely to be the strongest factors related to high performance in chess (e.g., see the Polgars). Moreover, chess players such as Kasparov and Judit Polgar have both been tested, and Kasparov scored 135 when he was tested by a magazine that employed psychologists. The strongest correlation is likely CPI compared to the rest.
